LENS: Statistical methodology
STANCE: Skeptical-but-fair

### Summary
The paper introduces the Diffusion Adapter Oracle (DAO), a language-model "interpreter" trained to read the raw weights of an image diffusion transformer's LoRA adapter — via a frozen, gauge-invariant bilinear sketch of each module's update injected as residual-stream tokens — and produce a natural-language description of the adapter's concept, without ever running the diffusion model. On a corpus of 831 minted adapters spanning 155 compositional concepts (rank/seed/module-set/image-set varied independently of concept), the trained interpreter names the held-out adapter's concept exactly in 55.2% of cases at 25 training epochs, versus 0% for a shuffled-token control and 13.3% for nearest-neighbour retrieval over the same tokens, with accuracy rising monotonically and not yet saturating across 6/12/25-epoch checkpoints. A secondary contribution compares three weight encoders and reports that the encoder that wins a small clamped-recipe retrieval test (subspace projectors, mAP ≈1.0) is the worst of the three at the corpus scale the interpreter actually trains on (3.7× chance), while the bilinear sketch used in the main pipeline, which loses the small test, wins at scale (11.0× chance).

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's third headline contribution — that the encoder ranking "inverts" between the small clamped-recipe test and the corpus-scale test — is never itself tested for statistical significance; only comparisons against chance/permutation nulls are reported.
   **Where** — Section 5 and Figure 2 ("At the interpreter's scale the ordering inverts"); the held-out accuracies compared are 0.092 (sketch), 0.061 (singular-direction detector), 0.031 (subspace projectors) over n=625 adapters / 98 held-out concepts.
   **Why it matters** — These three numbers are single point estimates from what appears to be one classifier fit per feature on one split. A 9.2% vs. 6.1% vs. 3.1% difference on a 98-way (or effectively per-concept binary) held-out task, with no confidence interval or paired test (e.g., bootstrap CI on the accuracy difference, or McNemar's test since the same held-out items presumably underlie all three classifiers), cannot be distinguished from sampling noise from the numbers given. The paper reports p-values for the clamped test (against a permutation null) but conspicuously does not report an analogous test for the scale-test ranking that is the whole point of the section. Since Contribution 3 is framed as a cautionary, generalizable methodological finding ("a caution about the smaller test, which is the one such features are usually validated on"), an unsupported ranking claim undermines a claim the paper explicitly wants readers to generalize from.
   **What would address it** — Report a bootstrap or permutation-based CI on each held-out accuracy, and a paired significance test between the three encoders' accuracies on the identical held-out set. If the differences are not significant at this scale, the "inversion" claim should be softened to "no longer distinguishable" rather than "inverts."

2. **Issue** — The scope of the Holm multiple-comparison correction is not fully specified, and there are strong signs of a large, only partially disclosed search over encoders, hyperparameters, and pipeline configurations before the reported result was obtained.
   **Where** — Appendix B ("Holm correction across the configurations tested"); Table 1 lists 8 configurations, but additional independently-reported p-values appear in Section 5 (permutation p=0.0005, 0.78, 0.92), Results (memorisation Fisher p=7.4×10⁻¹¹, cold-start p=7.8×10⁻⁴, warm-vs-cold p=1.0×10⁻⁵), and Appendix A references "four of our own failed runs" plus an earlier encoder that "recovers nothing."
   **Why it matters** — If these are all treated as independent, uncorrected exploratory tests outside the stated Holm family, the paper's actual multiplicity is considerably larger than "the configurations tested" implies, and the risk of a favourable result surviving through researcher degrees of freedom (which encoder, which epoch grid, which module-drop fix, which learning rate) is real — the paper itself documents encoder swaps (unit-normalised singular-direction tokens → bilinear sketch) and silent bugs (42.1% module drop, silently-overridden rank) discovered and fixed mid-project. Appendix B states a decision rule was "fixed before the deciding runs completed," which is good practice, but it is unclear exactly which comparisons that pre-registration covers (just Table 1's cross-LoRA/shuffled checks, or also the encoder comparison in Section 5).
   **What would address it** — State explicitly which statistical tests fall inside the pre-registered/Holm-corrected family and which are exploratory/post hoc, and report how many total encoder variants and pipeline configurations were tried before the reported bilinear-sketch pipeline was selected.

3. **Issue** — The central "accuracy rises with training budget and has not saturated" claim (Figure 3) rests on essentially one run per budget, and the single available replicate shows a large swing that is never statistically reconciled with the trend.
   **Where** — Figure 3 and Table 1: 12-epoch run gives 34.3% (36/105) while the independent 12-epoch replicate ("open marker") gives 25.7% (27/105) — an 8.6-percentage-point, ~25%-relative gap between two runs of the identical configuration.
   **Why it matters** — With run-to-run variance of that magnitude at a fixed budget, it is not established that the 12→25 epoch jump (34.3%→55.2%) or even the 6→12 jump (2.9%→34.3%) reflects a real monotonic trend rather than a noisy training process being sampled at three points. No confidence interval is given for any of the three main-line accuracies (55.2%, 34.3%, 2.9%), so the reader cannot assess whether "has not saturated" is a defensible extrapolation or an overread of three unreplicated point estimates.
   **What would address it** — Run and report multiple seeds at each epoch budget (as was done once for 12 epochs), with error bars/CIs (e.g., Wilson intervals on the n=105 proportions) on the epoch-accuracy curve, and a test of whether adjacent-budget differences exceed replicate noise.

4. **Issue** — The "published singular-direction detector" baseline used in the encoder comparison appears to be a reimplementation ("u1 + logistic regression"), not the original method run at its original operating point, and it is evaluated on a task quite different from the one it was designed and validated for.
   **Where** — Section 5 / Figure 2, referencing Africa et al. [2026]'s top-left singular-direction detector, described elsewhere (Section 2) as returning "a fixed label set" (e.g., harmful-vs-not) rather than open concept identification over 155/120 classes.
   **Why it matters** — Repurposing a binary/few-class detector's feature (u1) into a multiclass logistic-regression classifier and reporting that it under-performs a novel encoder is not necessarily a fair comparison of the original method's capability; the paper's claim that this comparison generalizes ("a caution about the smaller test") leans on the singular-direction feature being a faithful stand-in for that line of prior work.
   **What would address it** — Either cite this explicitly as "a singular-direction feature inspired by Africa et al." rather than implying it is their detector transplanted, or report the original detector's native task performance alongside the repurposed multiclass version for calibration.

5. **Issue** — No code or configuration release is mentioned for the DAO pipeline itself (encoder, injection, interpreter training scripts), only the corpus.
   **Where** — Contribution 2 / Section 4 ("We release the corpus... with the mint recipes"); no equivalent statement for the interpreter training code, the seeded projection matrices R, or the exact hyperparameter grids referenced in Appendix A (item 5: "the source configuration is tuned for roughly 1,900 examples with a warm start").
   **Why it matters** — Given how sensitive the reported results are to specific pipeline details the paper itself flags as easy to get silently wrong (module-drop bugs, rank-override bugs, learning-rate/step-budget mismatches), independent reproduction is not really possible from the corpus alone. This matters more than usual here because several of the paper's own internal replications (Appendix A) turned out to be misconfigurations rather than results.
   **What would address it** — State (or add) a code release for the encoder, injection, and training pipeline, including the seeded R matrices and the exact hyperparameters for each row of Table 1.

### Minor concerns
- The abstract and title ("names the concept of a held-out adapter") could be skimmed as concept-level generalization; the paper's own Limitations section clarifies this is recipe generalization on trained concepts, not generalization to unseen concepts — worth surfacing this caveat one sentence earlier, in the abstract itself, rather than only via the later qualifying sentence.
- p-values as extreme as 5.2×10⁻²³ (25-epoch vs. its control, which sits at literally 0/105) are close to guaranteed by the control's degenerate zero rate and add little beyond what the raw counts already show; effect-size/CI reporting would be more informative than further decimal places of significance.
- "×chance" framing (e.g., 85.6× chance in Table 1) is an inflation-prone way to report effect size when chance itself is tiny (1/155); absolute accuracy with a CI is more interpretable and is only inconsistently given alongside the ratio.
- One-sided Fisher tests are used throughout (Appendix B) without explicit justification for why two-sided would be inappropriate, despite the interpreter's actual behavior (e.g., cross-LoRA control) not being guaranteed a priori to be non-negative in effect.
- The gap statistic "59.2% of gaps below 10⁻²" (Section 2) is reported without specifying over which modules/adapters it was computed or how "gap" is operationalised, making it hard to independently verify the ill-conditioning argument it supports.
- No broader-impact/ethics statement despite the tool's explicit motivating use case (screening model hubs for unanticipated capabilities, and the related work citing a CSAM-adapter detector); a short statement on dual-use risk of a general concept-naming oracle would be appropriate given the stated motivation.
- LoRAcle [De Schamphelaere et al., 2026], the closest prior work and primary architectural template, is only available via an ICML workshop OpenReview link rather than arXiv, so its claims (e.g., single-question-setting collapse, unnormalised-injection divergence) could not be independently cross-checked in this review.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the core result (interpreter reads adapter weights above matched controls) is well-controlled and plausible, but the paper's own secondary headline claim (encoder ranking "inverts" at scale) and its training-budget trend are asserted without the significance tests or replicate variance needed to support them, and the scope of multiple-comparison correction across the many reported tests is unclear.
