### Summary

The paper trains a 14B-parameter language model to read the frozen weights of an image-diffusion LoRA adapter (via a gauge-invariant bilinear sketch injected into the residual stream) and name the adapter's trained concept in free text, reporting 55.2% exact-match accuracy on held-out adapters of seen concepts against 0% for a shuffled-token control and 13.3% for nearest-neighbor retrieval. It also reports an encoder comparison in which the ranking of candidate weight-space features inverts between a small clamped-recipe test and the interpreter's actual operating scale, and a self-critical appendix of diagnostic checks derived from the authors' own failed runs. The self-honesty is real and unusually good for the genre (explicit statement that the held-out split generalizes across training recipe, not to unseen concepts; an entire appendix devoted to distinguishing broken pipelines from negative results). But the statistical case for the headline number is thinner than the profusion of p-values suggests: the 25-epoch headline configuration is a single run with no replicate, the encoder that was locked in for the headline model was chosen on a smaller, different corpus snapshot than the one it was ultimately used on, and — most concretely — Table 1's own data contradicts the paper's textual claim that the cross-adapter control "falls to zero at every budget." The evidence is suggestive of a real effect, but not as airtight as the framing implies.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper never runs the natural head-to-head baseline: a cheap linear or kNN classifier on the same bilinear-sketch features, evaluated on the *same* held-out split used for the 55.2% headline number. **[Evidence — baselines]**
   **Where** — Section 5 vs. Section 6; Table 1.
   **Why it matters** — Section 5's classifier-on-sketch-features result (0.092, 11.0× chance) is measured on a different, smaller corpus vintage (625 adapters, 98 held out at 120 concepts) than the headline interpreter result (764 adapters, 105 held out at 155 concepts). Without the matched comparison, a reader cannot tell how much of the 55.2% is bought by the trained 14B-parameter interpreter versus already present in the frozen features. Related work even flags the natural comparator — Putterman et al.'s GL-equivariant architecture for exactly this weight-space gauge problem — is discussed at length in Section 2 but never entered as a competing encoder in Section 5's comparison, despite that comparison existing specifically to litigate which encoder is best.
   **What would address it** — Report the linear/kNN-on-sketch-features baseline on the identical 764/155/105 split used for the headline claim, and include the GL-equivariant encoder in the Section 5 comparison or explain why it was excluded.

2. **Issue** — Every one of the 831 evaluated adapters is self-minted by the authors on a shared toolchain with known, templated concepts; no independently-authored, real Hugging Face adapter is ever scored. **[Limitations — external validity / Narrative — overclaiming]**
   **Where** — Section 4 (corpus description); Limitations (Section 7, not mentioned there).
   **Why it matters** — The intro's entire motivation is screening the 129,533 real LoRA adapters on the Hub for "capabilities nobody anticipated," and the title claims interpretability "at Scale." But the interpreter has only ever seen adapters from one minting pipeline (ai-toolkit, FLUX.2-klein-4B, one templated compositional-concept scheme). We have no evidence it generalizes to adapters trained with different code, different base models, merged/quantized adapters, or organically-drifted concepts — exactly the setting the intro uses to motivate the work. This is a bigger gap than most of the eight limitations actually listed (Section 7), yet it's absent from that list.
   **What would address it** — Score the trained interpreter on even a small sample of real, independently-sourced Hub LoRAs with human-verified concept labels, or explicitly add this as a stated limitation rather than omitting it.

3. **Issue** — The two verbatim model outputs shown in Section 6 are both correct/near-correct; no random sample of held-out generations — success or failure — is shown. **[Evidence — cherry-picking]**
   **Where** — Section 6, "What the interpreter says."
   **Why it matters** — 44.8% of held-out adapters are named wrongly by the paper's own numbers, and the paper itself claims the errors are "structured" (medium attribute wrong most often, Figure 4). Two hand-picked hits don't let a reader judge whether the ~45% of failures look like plausible partial generalization (e.g., right family/subject, wrong medium) or unrelated confabulation — which matters a lot for whether "0.427 mean attribute credit" is an honest characterization of near-misses or a generous scoring artifact.
   **What would address it** — Show a small random sample (not hand-picked) of held-out generations, including outright misses, alongside ground truth.

4. **Issue** — Several stated architectural choices are never ablated for DAO itself: the injection layer, the per-module identity embedding, and the multi-question supervision scheme. **[Evidence — ablations]**
   **Where** — Section 3 ("Injection," "Supervision and interpreter").
   **Why it matters** — The paper justifies the multi-question scheme and the normalized injection by pointing to what happened in a *different* paper (LoRAcle: "a single-question setting collapsed in the source work"; "an unnormalised version diverges, which LoRAcle also reports"). Neither is demonstrated to hold for this cross-architecture setting, where the reader has different width/vocabulary from the read model — the justification for two design choices is borrowed from a setting the paper itself says is architecturally different from its own (same-architecture text-adapter reading vs. cross-architecture diffusion-adapter reading).
   **What would address it** — At minimum, one ablation confirming the single-question collapse and injection normalization findings replicate in the cross-architecture DAO setting rather than citing the analogous text-model result.

5. **Issue** — No repository/code link, injection layer index, projection-matrix seed, or exact training-target strings are given; only the corpus (adapters + mint recipes) is stated as released. **[Reproducibility]**
   **Where** — Section 3 (encoder/injection description); Contributions list; References (De Schamphelaere et al. entry lists a GitHub repo and checkpoint name for the *prior* paper, DAO's own artifacts are not similarly listed).
   **Why it matters** — A competent grad student could mint the corpus (recipe is given) but could not reproduce the interpreter: "a fixed layer" for injection is never given a number, "seeded" random projections R never state the seed, and the exact target strings used for supervision are never shown (only two example *outputs*, not training targets). Given the paper leans on a warm-started checkpoint from another lab's released work, the asymmetry — borrow open infrastructure, don't state release plans for your own — is worth flagging explicitly rather than leaving implicit.
   **What would address it** — State the injection layer index, projection seed(s), and either release code/checkpoint or explicitly note it is withheld and why.

6. **Issue** — The claim "Feeding a trained interpreter a different adapter's tokens drops it to 0%... accuracy falls to zero at every budget" is contradicted by the paper's own Table 1: at 6 epochs, the cross-LoRA control scores 4/105, *higher* than the true condition's own 3/105 (2.9%) at that same budget — and there is no 6-epoch shuffled-token control row in the table at all, so the abstract's blanket claim "every matched control at zero" is not actually backed by data at the 6-epoch point it explicitly cites. **[Evidence — statistical rigour]**
   **Where** — Table 1, row "6 epochs" (Cross-LoRA = 4/105); Section 6 ("accuracy falls to zero at every budget"); Abstract ("2.9%, 34.3% and 55.2% at 6, 12 and 25 epochs, with every matched control at zero").
   **Why it matters** — Steelmanned, the paper's overall statistical case is genuinely above the interpretability-paper norm: real negative controls (shuffled tokens, cross-adapter, no-injection), a retrieval baseline, monotonic scaling with training budget, and Holm-corrected Fisher tests with very small p-values against controls fixed at exactly zero. But pressing on it: (a) a control literally *beating* the true condition at the lowest budget, in the exact table meant to demonstrate the control always sits at floor, is either a genuine anomaly worth explaining or evidence the 6-epoch "signal" (2.9%, only ~4.4× a 0.65% chance rate) is itself noise indistinguishable from the control — the paper cannot have it both ways by citing 2.9% as a real, scaling data point in the abstract while its own cross-LoRA column at that budget undermines the "controls are always zero" claim used to license treating any nonzero number as signal; (b) the headline 25-epoch configuration (55.2%) is a single run with no replicate, while the one epoch-count that *is* replicated (12 epochs) swings from 34.3% to 25.7% — an ~9-point absolute swing between nominally identical runs — with no variance estimate (bootstrap CI, multiple seeds) ever placed on the point estimate itself, only p-values against a control that is trivially at floor for an exact-match metric on open-ended generation; (c) the encoder locked in for the headline model (Section 3's bilinear sketch) was selected via a ranking (Section 5) run on an earlier, smaller corpus (625/120) than the one the headline model was ultimately trained and evaluated on (764/155) — and the paper's own showcased finding is that this exact ranking *inverted* once already between two different scales, which is direct evidence the ranking is scale-unstable, yet it is never re-checked at the third (final, headline) scale before being committed to.
   **What would address it** — Explain or correct the 6-epoch cross-LoRA anomaly and reconcile it with the abstract's "every matched control at zero" claim; run at least 2–3 seeds at the 25-epoch headline configuration and report a variance estimate (not just a p-value against a floor control) on the 55.2% figure; re-verify the encoder ranking at the final 764/155 corpus scale before treating Section 5's choice as settled.

### Minor concerns

- Table 1 apparent contradiction from concern 6 is compounded by ambiguity in what "Rank" (the retrieval-rank column) is actually computed against — the text doesn't fully spell out the retrieval procedure used to produce this column, only that "0.5 is chance and lower is better."
- Appendix A, item 4 states "Our memorisation comparison scored 0.231 at 13 concepts and 0.029 at 120" — a "13 concepts" split appears nowhere else in the paper (the two named scales are 8/32-adapter clamped test and 120/155-concept full corpus splits); this reads as an orphaned number.
- Footnote 1's Hub adapter count (129,533) is a live, unarchived figure ("retrieved 26 August 2026") with no URL or snapshot — it will drift and can't be checked by a reader.
- Appendix B states Fisher's exact tests are one-sided; one-sided tests against a floor-zero control inflate apparent significance relative to a two-sided test and are worth flagging explicitly as a choice, not just stating it in passing.
- Figure 2's rendered multiplier annotations ("0.031 (3.7£)", "0.061 (7.3£)") appear to be a "×" symbol garbled to "£" — likely a font/encoding issue in the source PDF rather than an authoring error, but worth the authors double-checking the camera-ready render.
- No compute/hardware details (GPU type/hours) are given for the ~40-minutes-per-adapter minting cost or interpreter training, making cost claims ("expensive part") hard to contextualize.
- The exact training-target strings (ground truth used for supervision) are never shown — only two example model *outputs* — making it hard to judge how generous exact-match scoring is in practice.

### Belief update

Modest upward update on the narrow proposition that a language model can extract some concept-relevant signal from an image-diffusion LoRA's raw weights, without running the diffusion model, better than a shuffled-weights or nearest-neighbor-retrieval baseline — the no-injection and cross-LoRA controls (setting aside the 6-epoch anomaly) and the retrieval baseline do rule out the most obvious "it's reading the prompt, not the weights" and "it's memorizing the training set" confounds. Essentially no update on the broader claim implied by the framing (interpretability "at scale," screening real Hub adapters for unanticipated capabilities) — the entire evaluation is self-minted, in-distribution, single-toolchain data, so nothing here bears on the deployment scenario motivating the paper. No update on novelty — this is a fairly direct extension of LoRAcle's same-architecture recipe to a cross-architecture setting via a gauge-invariant sketch, which is a reasonable but incremental engineering contribution, not a new idea.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the internal contradiction between the abstract's "every matched control at zero" claim and Table 1's own 6-epoch cross-LoRA row (4/105, beating the true condition), combined with the absence of any variance estimate or replicate at the single-run 25-epoch headline configuration, means the paper's central quantitative claim is less statistically settled than its dense apparatus of p-values suggests.
