# Review (Reviewer bravo)

### Summary

The paper asks whether a language model can be trained to read the frozen weights of an image-diffusion LoRA adapter — without ever running the diffusion model — and state in natural language what concept the adapter encodes. The authors build the Diffusion Adapter Oracle (DAO): a frozen, gauge-invariant bilinear-sketch encoder turns each adapter module into one token at a Qwen3-14B interpreter's residual width, the tokens are injected into the interpreter's residual stream, and the interpreter is fine-tuned (LoRA) to answer questions about the adapter. To supply training data, the authors mint 831 LoRA adapters over 155 compositional concepts for FLUX.2-klein-4B, varying rank/seed/module-set/image-set independently of concept. The headline result: on held-out adapter instances (unseen training recipe, but of a concept the interpreter saw other instances of), exact-match concept naming reaches 55.2% at 25 epochs, against 0% for a shuffled-token control and a cross-adapter control, and against 13.3% for nearest-neighbour retrieval over the same tokens — with accuracy rising monotonically and not yet saturating across the epoch budgets tested (2.9% → 34.3% → 55.2%). A secondary contribution reports that the weight encoder that wins a small "clamped-recipe" retrieval test (subspace projectors) is the worst encoder at the scale the interpreter actually operates at, where a bilinear sketch of the full update wins instead, and a diagnostic protocol (Appendix A) is offered for distinguishing genuine negative results from misconfiguration.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's headline framing ("names the concept," "reads adapter weights and says what it does") describes something closer to closed-set classification over a known, compositional label vocabulary than open-ended understanding, and the evidence presented cannot distinguish the two.
   **Where** — Abstract; Section 1, Contributions item 1; Section 6 "What the interpreter says"; partially walked back in Section 7 ("we do not claim that setting").
   **Why it matters** — Held-out adapters are, by the paper's own definition, unseen *instances* of concepts the interpreter was trained on ("Held-out adapters are ones the interpreter has never seen, trained at a different rank, seed and module set... so the task is generalisation across training recipe rather than to unseen concepts"). Because concepts are compositional (family × object × medium × palette) and drawn from a small, fixed attribute vocabulary reused across the 155 concepts, every word that can appear in a held-out label was already seen during training, just in other combinations. The strongest evidence offered for "reading the weights" — the cross-LoRA control dropping to 0% and the shuffled-token control staying at 0% — establishes that the output is *causally dependent on which adapter's weights are injected*, but it does not discriminate between "the interpreter recovers compositional semantic content" and "the interpreter performs coarse 1-of-155 bucket classification from a recipe-invariant signature." Figure 4's attribute breakdown (family 0.80, easiest, explicitly "takes few values") is more consistent with the latter than the paper's rhetorical framing suggests. This matters because the title ("Verbalizing Image LoRAs for Interpretability at Scale") and abstract promise a capability — describing *what an adapter does* at Hub scale — that is not the capability actually validated (matching a new instance against a closed set of previously-labeled concepts).
   **What would address it** — State the closed-set nature of the task plainly in the abstract and contributions list, not only in Limitations. Report chance rates and effective cardinality per attribute axis (how many distinct family/medium/palette values exist and how they distribute across the 155 concepts) so a reader can judge how much of "family=0.80" is driven by a small label space. Ideally, include a genuine held-out-*concept* (zero-shot) evaluation — the paper mentions a "held-out family" split exists (Appendix B) but reports it "only for completeness" without numbers; those numbers are the single most informative thing missing from the paper for this claim.

2. **Issue** — No evaluation on real, in-the-wild Hugging Face LoRA adapters, despite that being the paper's explicit motivating deployment scenario.
   **Where** — Section 1 (motivation: "Hugging Face hosts 129,533 model repositories tagged as LoRA adapters... Anyone screening a hub at that scale... cannot afford to run every adapter"); absent from Sections 4–6.
   **Why it matters** — Every adapter DAO is trained and tested on is self-minted with a single toolkit (ai-toolkit), a single base model (FLUX.2-klein-4B), and known, benign, curated concepts. The paper's own related-work discussion (Africa et al. 2026 on CSAM-adapter detection, Puertolas Merenciano et al. 2026 on backdoors) frames the target use case as adapters with unanticipated, possibly adversarial content on a heterogeneous public hub. Nothing here demonstrates the encoder or interpreter transfers to adapters trained with different toolkits, ranks/alphas outside the minted recipe grid, different base architectures, or genuinely unknown concepts — which is precisely the "capabilities they did not anticipate" setting the paper is pitched to solve. The gap between the stated problem and the tested setting is large enough to bear directly on whether the headline number supports the paper's claimed use case.
   **What would address it** — Even a small qualitative case study applying the trained interpreter to a handful of real public Hub LoRAs (with human-graded outputs) would substantially strengthen external validity; absent that, the claims should be scoped explicitly to the minted-corpus, closed-set setting.

3. **Issue** — The headline number lacks a confidence interval, and the one replicate available at the 12-epoch setting shows run-to-run variance large enough to call the "rising, not yet saturated" narrative into question.
   **Where** — Table 1 ("12 epochs": 36/105 = 34.3% vs. "12 epochs, repeat": 27/105 = 25.7%); Figure 3 (open marker described as "an independent run of the 12-epoch configuration"); no CI reported anywhere in Section 6 or Table 1, in contrast to Figure 2's bootstrap intervals.
   **Why it matters** — Two nominally identical 12-epoch runs differ by 8.6 percentage points (34.3% vs. 25.7%), roughly one binomial standard error at n=105. The 25-epoch headline figure (55.2%) has only a single run — no replicate is reported at that budget — so its precision is unknown, and it is entirely possible that a repeat at 25 epochs would land anywhere in a similarly wide band. The smooth-looking monotonic curve in Figure 3 (2.9% → 34.3%/25.7% → 55.2%) is doing a lot of narrative work ("the reported figure is a lower bound," "accuracy rises with the training budget and has not saturated") on data that, by the paper's own repeat measurement, contains substantial run-to-run noise.
   **What would address it** — Report multiple seeds at each epoch budget (at minimum at 25 epochs, the headline number), with binomial or bootstrap confidence intervals on every point in Figure 3 and every accuracy cell in Table 1.

4. **Issue** — The exact-match grading procedure that produces the headline 55.2% is never specified.
   **Where** — Section 6, "What the interpreter says" and the surrounding accuracy claims; Section 7 ("Accuracy is scored by exact concept match").
   **Why it matters** — The qualitative examples shown are free-text generations ("Honestly, I steer everything toward ukiyo e woodblock...", "A concept adapter for gen object fern frond etched metal mono contrast."). Turning such outputs into a binary match/no-match judgment against a target label requires a grading rule (string containment? normalized token match? an LLM judge?) that is never described. Since the entire headline statistic — and every Fisher's-exact comparison built on it — depends on this undisclosed step, a reader cannot assess how lenient or strict the grading is, nor reproduce it.
   **What would address it** — Specify the exact grading algorithm (ideally release the grading script alongside the corpus), and report inter-rater or grader-vs-human agreement if any judgment calls are involved.

5. **Issue** — The paper compares the interpreter against nearest-neighbour retrieval and independent per-feature linear probes, but not against a single stronger supervised classifier (e.g., a small nonlinear model) trained end-to-end on the same sketch tokens to predict concept identity.
   **Where** — Section 6, "It beats memorisation" (NN retrieval 13.3%); Section 5 (per-feature linear classifiers, 9.2% for the sketch at a different split: 625/98 held-out, chance 0.83%).
   **Why it matters** — The 11.0×-chance linear-probe number from Section 5 and the 13.3% NN-retrieval number from Section 6 are measured on different splits (98 vs. 105 held-out, different corpus snapshots per Section 4), so they are only loosely comparable to the interpreter's 34.3–55.2%. A held-out accuracy gap of this size between a linear/NN reader and a 14B-parameter fine-tuned language model is exactly what you would also expect from raw capacity/nonlinearity even absent any "semantic reading" ability. Without a matched-split nonlinear baseline (e.g., a gradient-boosted classifier or small MLP head on the identical tokens, evaluated on the identical 105 held-out set), the paper cannot cleanly attribute its margin to the interpreter's language-model machinery rather than to model capacity.
   **What would address it** — Add a nonlinear (non-language-model) classifier baseline on the exact same held-out split used for the headline number.

6. **Issue** — No ethics/dual-use statement, despite the paper's own motivation explicitly invoking harmful-content detection as a use case.
   **Where** — Absent throughout; Section 1 and Section 2 cite CSAM-adapter detection (Africa et al. 2026) and backdoor detection (Puertolas Merenciano et al. 2026) as the class of problem DAO generalizes beyond.
   **Why it matters** — A tool that verbalizes what an arbitrary LoRA's weights encode without running the model is dual-use: the same capability that helps a defender triage an unfamiliar Hub adapter could help an attacker efficiently catalogue or curate weights for undesired content while avoiding execution-based review pipelines. Given the paper explicitly motivates itself against a CSAM-detection paper, some discussion of misuse potential and safeguards (or an explicit statement that none is needed and why) is warranted.
   **What would address it** — Add a short ethics/broader-impact paragraph addressing dual-use risk and any mitigations (e.g., controlled release of the trained interpreter checkpoint vs. open release of only the corpus).

7. **Issue** — Reproducibility commitments are incomplete: only the corpus and mint recipes are stated as released; nothing is said about the interpreter training code, encoder implementation, trained checkpoints, or the provenance/licensing of the images used to mint each adapter.
   **Where** — Section 4 ("We release the corpus... with the mint recipes"); no statement elsewhere about code/checkpoint release; Section 4 mentions "the image set it was trained on" without describing image source or licensing.
   **Why it matters** — Given the corpus is the expensive part of this work (the paper itself says so — "an image adapter costs about forty minutes to mint"), a reader who wants to verify Table 1/Figure 3 needs the interpreter training pipeline and grading script, not only the adapters. Image provenance also matters for anyone trying to reuse or extend the corpus responsibly.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly (post-anonymity-period) whether interpreter training code, encoder code, trained checkpoints, and image-source licensing will be released.

### Minor concerns

- Figure 2's transcribed values ("u1 + logistic regression," bare numbers "1.00 / 0.71 / 0.91" floating above the panel) are difficult to parse from the paper text as extracted; if this reflects the actual typeset figure rather than an extraction artifact, the labeling needs cleanup.
- The load-bearing "95% of a sample of 10,000 image-generation checkpoints were LoRAs" statistic (Section 1) is sourced to a blog post (Bossan et al. 2026); given how much motivational weight it carries, a firmer methodological citation would help.
- Chance-rate denominators shift across sections (1/155 in Section 6/Figure 3, 1/120 in Section 5, different held-out counts of 98/105) without a single summary table — a reader has to reconstruct which experiment used which corpus snapshot.
- Figure 4's n=86 "compositional held-out adapters" is not reconciled arithmetically against the n=105 total held-out set used elsewhere; state how the 86 are selected from the 105.
- Related work on separating style from content within LoRA weights directly, e.g. B-LoRA's implicit style–content separation for diffusion adapters [2403.14572], is not discussed, though it bears on why "medium" (rendering style) is reported as the hardest attribute to recover (Figure 4) — worth a sentence of context even though that work doesn't verbalize its output.
- The dense-vs-sketch extraction speedup ("about 223 times slower... infeasible") is stated without units (adapters/sec? wall-clock for the full corpus?), making it hard to judge practical significance.
- "We measured 59.2% of gaps below 10⁻²" (Section 2, gauge-symmetry discussion) does not state which corpus/sample this measurement was taken over, or how the spectral gap was aggregated across modules/layers.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the controlled-comparison evidence (matched shuffled/cross-adapter controls, Holm-corrected significance) genuinely supports that trained-interpreter output depends causally on adapter weights and beats naive retrieval, but the paper's framing overstates a closed-set, in-distribution-concept classification result as general "verbalizing what an adapter does," omits any test on the real-world Hub adapters that motivate the work, and leaves the headline number without a confidence interval despite visible run-to-run variance in the one replicate reported.
